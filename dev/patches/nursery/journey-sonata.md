## Opening

Big question: *How did a word that first meant "a piece played, not sung" become the name of one of the great plans of Western music?*

What a sonata is today (one sentence): Today a sonata is usually a piece for one or two instruments in several movements, with a first movement built in **sonata form**. This journey stops in 1740, before that plan is complete, so what you meet along the way will not look like that.

## Root: 1597 · Polychoral music (the lantern: 1550)

Where the journey starts. The lantern is dated 1550; its starred work here is from 1597. Core work for every stage.

**What "sonata" means here.**
- Stage 4: Here "sonata" only means a piece played by instruments, not sung. The players are split into two groups that answer each other.
- Stage 5: Not yet a form (**sonatas**). In Gabrieli's 1597 title it marks a piece for instruments. It has no written **basso continuo** part. The idea behind it is **cori spezzati**, groups placed apart that answer each other and then combine. Giovanni Gabrieli was organist at St Mark's Basilica in Venice, and he names the instruments in his score, which was unusual at the time.
- Stage 6: The Garden's card says that in 1550 Willaert published psalms for two choirs (salmi spezzati) in Venice, and that Gabrieli's *Sacrae symphoniae* (1597) mixes choirs of voices and instruments. The canzona card adds that the *Sacrae symphoniae* apply polychoral writing to instruments, and that this sonata names its instruments (cornett, violin, sackbuts) and marks piano and forte. It is among the first pieces to mark **dynamics** in the score.

**What changed.** Where it starts.

**The work.** *Sonata pian'e forte*, Giovanni Gabrieli, 1597.
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PcRSJz32kTs (His Majestys Sagbutts & Cornetts)

**Listen for.**
- (dynamics) sudden changes between soft and loud. *Stages 4, 5, 6.*
- (texture) two groups answering each other, then combining. *Stages 4, 5, 6.*
- (performing media) one group led by a **cornett**, the other by a low violin (close to a viola), each with three **sackbut** players. *Stages 5, 6.*

**Find out by listening.**
- Stage 4: Hold up a card marked p (soft) or f (loud) each time the sound changes. How many groups are playing each time?
- Stage 5: When the two groups answer each other, can you tell them apart by sound? What helps you tell them apart?
- Stage 6: How do the sudden changes between soft and loud (**terraced dynamics**) and the choice of instruments create contrast between the two groups?

**New words.** **cori spezzati**, **terraced dynamics**, **cornett**, **sackbut**.
**Check question.** When the sound is soft, do you hear all of the players or only some of them?
**For teachers.** The Garden's canzona entry reads *Sacrae symphoniae: Sonata pian' e forte*. The recording is titled "alla quarta bassa", which means played a fourth lower than written. A viola-range violin is audible in this recording (checked by ear, 22 Nov).

## Chapter 1. 1613 · Canzona & early sonata (the lantern: c.1580)

The Garden links Polychoral music to this lantern ("influenced"). Stages 5 and 6: the first work here, Rossi's, is 16 years after Gabrieli's. Castello's is 8 years after Rossi's and 24 years after Gabrieli's.

**What "sonata" means here.**
- Stage 4: Now "sonata" means a piece for a few players, in short parts that change speed. The big groups are gone.
- Stage 5: Pieces for instruments (**sonatas**, **canzona**), usually written over a **basso continuo** from about 1610; Gabrieli's 1597 sonata has no written continuo part. Castello's first book (Venice, 1621) has no solo sonatas: Nos. 1 to 8 are for two parts and Nos. 9 to 12 for three. The Garden's recording is Sonata VIII, for violin and bassoon with continuo. Castello builds his piece from short sections that change speed and metre without stopping. Rossi (court violinist at Mantua) takes a different approach: the Garden's card says he repeats a well-known bass pattern under changing upper parts. His 1613 score is for two viole da braccio (bowed string instruments) and a chitarrone, a bass instrument built to play continuo (**theorbo**, also called chitarrone).
- Stage 6: The Garden's card says the ensemble canzona grew from instrumental arrangements of the Parisian chanson, and that sectional design (contrasting tempos and metres in one span) is the seed of the multi-movement sonata. It describes Castello's *sonate concertate* as lines set against a **basso continuo**, which is a different make-up from Gabrieli's two choirs.

**What changed since the root.**
- Stage 4: Two big groups have become two players, with a bass part underneath.
- Stage 5: The two big choirs shrink to two melody instruments, with a **basso continuo** (or, in Rossi, a bass instrument built for it) filling in underneath.
- Stage 6: The writing moves from choirs of instruments answering each other (a change of texture and **performing media**) to two lines over a bass. Inside this chapter the plan differs: Rossi builds his piece over one repeating bass pattern, while Castello strings together short sections that change speed and metre.

**1. 1613 · Sonata sopra l'aria di Ruggiero, Salamone Rossi.** *Stages 5 and 6.*
The Garden's canzona entry gives the title as *Sonata sopra l'aria di Ruggiero* (*Il terzo libro de varie sonate*).
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_ML1JTCBOv4 (Musica Antiqua Köln)
Listen for:
- (structure) a bass pattern that keeps repeating while the upper parts change.
- (performing media) two melody instruments over a bass instrument.
Find out by listening:
- Stage 5: Which instruments can you hear in this recording? Does it match the title page (two bowed instruments and a chitarrone)?
- Stage 6: Follow the lowest line. What does it do while the upper parts change, and how is that different from Castello's way of making variety?
For teachers: The recording is Musica Antiqua Köln, directed by Reinhard Goebel (Archiv Produktion, 1985; track 4, credited to Salamone Rossi, 3:38), uploaded to YouTube by the label's Topic channel. The Garden's linked score is the *Sonata quarta* (Book 3, Op. 12, No. 4); the title page reads "Per sonar due Viole da braccio, & un Chittarone, o altro stromento simile". The repeating bass is the Garden card's statement; it has not been checked by ear in this recording, and neither has the line-up.
*Notice:* in the next work, 8 years on, the bass no longer repeats a pattern; the music changes speed and metre.

**2. 1621 · *Sonate concertate in stil moderno, libro primo* (Sonata VIII), Dario Castello.** *Core work for every stage.*
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Du3vbk6op_s

Listen for:
- (texture) two melody lines, one high and one low, over continuo. *Stages 5, 6.*
- (performing media) a high violin and a low bassoon, one bowed and one blown. *Stages 4, 5, 6.*
- (structure) short sections changing speed and metre. *Stages 4, 5, 6.*

Find out by listening:
- Stage 4: Step to the beat. Every time the speed or the feel changes, change how you move. How many times did you change?
- Stage 5: How many times does the speed or metre change?
- Stage 6: Mark each point where the tempo or metre changes. What does that tell you about how the piece is built, and how is it different from Gabrieli's way of making contrast?
For teachers: The recording is from Torculus records; the performers are not named. About 10 changes of speed or metre were counted in Sonata VIII (22 Nov). Castello's work is 24 years after Gabrieli's.

## Chapter 2. 1629 · Trio sonata & solo sonata (the lantern: c.1610)

The Garden links Canzona & early sonata to this lantern ("path"). Stages 5 and 6: the first work here, Marini's, is 8 years after Castello's.

**What "sonata" means here.**
- Stage 4: Now the main players are violins. Two violins talk to each other over a bass. Listen for how the music is split into slow and fast parts.
- Stage 5: The meaning shifts during this lantern. It begins (Marini, 1629) as a string of sections for two violins over continuo. *La Monica* ("the nun") was a popular song, and the Garden's card says Marini's sonata is built on it, varied section by section. By the 1680s Corelli has set two plans: the **sonata da chiesa** and the **sonata da camera**. Corelli's Op. 1 (Rome, 1681) holds church sonatas; his Op. 2 (1685) does the same for the chamber sonata, a set of dances. The **trio sonata** has three parts, usually played by four players.
- Stage 6: The Garden's card says Corelli's Opp. 1 to 4 (1681 to 94) standardised the two types: four-movement church sonatas (slow, fast, slow, fast, often with a fugal second movement) and chamber sonatas of stylised dances. The lantern also holds solo sonatas, such as the twelfth by Isabella Leonarda, an Ursuline nun of Novara, whom the Garden calls the first woman known to publish instrumental sonatas (1693). Her Op. 16 (Bologna, 1693) has 12 sonatas; Nos. 1 to 11 are for two violins, violone and organ, and No. 12 is her only solo sonata.

**What changed since Chapter 1.**
- Stage 4 (Corelli only): The bassoon has gone. Now it is two violins and a bass.
- Stage 5 (Marini and Corelli): Both are written for two violins over a continuo bass, 52 years apart. Marini strings sections together in one span; Corelli's Op. 1 separates them into movements.
- Stage 6 (all three): The melody instruments are violins over a continuo bass: two in Marini (1629) and Corelli (1681), one in Leonarda (1693). Inside this chapter the plan changes twice. Marini's piece is a string of sections built on a returning tune; by Corelli's Op. 1 the sections are separated into movements; and Leonarda's sonata turns to a single violin, with free, improvised-sounding passages alternating with steadier sections.

**1. 1629 · Sonata "sopra la Monica", Op. 8 (No. 45), Biagio Marini.** *Stages 5 and 6.*
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ld5E7YO33fg (Il Giardino Armonico)
Listen for: (structure) the opening tune keeps coming back, changed each time; (texture) two high instruments over a continuo bass.
Find out by listening: Which instruments play the top parts in this recording?
For teachers: The score (Op. 8 No. 45) is for two violins and continuo. This recording probably uses both violins and other high instruments, which is not confirmed.
*Notice (Stages 5 and 6):* in the next work, 52 years on, listen for how the music is divided.

**2. 1681 · *Trio Sonatas, Op. 1*, Arcangelo Corelli.** *Core work for every stage.*
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bk5qRo32eB0 (London Baroque; Op. 1 No. 10, in G minor)
Listen for:
- (texture) the second violin entering with the first violin's tune, an example of **imitation**. *Stages 5, 6.*
- (duration) a walking bass: steady steps in the bass under busy upper parts. *Stages 4, 5, 6.*
Find out by listening:
- Stage 4: Show with your hands how fast the music feels. Does it stay the same, or does it change?
- Stage 5: (a) Play a G on a piano or a tuner app while the recording plays. Does it match? (b) How is the music divided into movements, and how do they differ in speed?
- Stage 6: The Garden's card describes a church sonata as four movements, slow, fast, slow, fast, often with a fugal second movement. Does this sonata match that description? Compare the opening of each movement: what changes and what is kept?
For teachers: The video title says D minor, but this is Op. 1 No. 10 in G minor. London Baroque sound about a semitone low (near G-flat minor), which fits Baroque pitch (A = 415, not 440); this is by ear, not from a documented source. The movements are Grave, Allegro, Adagio, Allegro (Chandos booklet CH0692).
*Notice (Stage 6):* in the next work, 12 years on, the sound thins to one violin over continuo.

**3. 1693 · Sonata duodecima, Op. 16, Isabella Leonarda.** *Stage 6.*
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w1GSPwEz4Kw (Manami Mizumoto, baroque violin, Voices of Music)
Listen for: (texture) one violin over continuo; (structure) free, improvised-sounding passages alternating with steadier sections in a clear beat.
Find out by listening: (a) Which instruments play the continuo underneath the violin? (b) Which passages sound free, and which sound steady?
For teachers: The free and steady alternation follows the West Cork Chamber Music Festival notes on this sonata. The continuo instruments in the recording have not been identified.

**New words.** **trio sonata**, **sonata da chiesa**, **sonata da camera**.
**Check question.**
- Stages 4 and 5: In a fast movement of the Corelli, does one violin start alone? When does the other come in, and does it copy the same tune?
- Stage 6: Is Leonarda's sonata closer to Marini's or to Corelli's in how it is built? Say why, from what you hear.
**Find out by listening.**
- Stage 4: Listen to the whole Corelli. Where does the bass move most, and where least?
- Stage 5: Which sounds more sectional, Marini or Corelli, and which more settled? What in the sound made you choose?
- Stage 6: Put Marini, Corelli and Leonarda in order from most sectional to most settled. What in the sound made you choose that order?
**For teachers.** These questions are open. Accept answers that give a reason from the sound.

## Chapter 3. 1732 · Keyboard sonata (the lantern: c.1735)

**A leap, not a link.** No Garden link joins the trio sonata to this lantern. It crosses from Chamber to Keyboard, a gap of 39 years from Leonarda's sonata (1693) to Giustini's (1732), and about 45 years to Scarlatti's. Sonatas for violin and continuo went on being written in these years (Corelli's Op. 5, 1700), but the Garden's next sonata is for keyboard. Two Garden links lead into this lantern, from **galant** and from the dance **suite**, both "influenced".

**What "sonata" means here.**
- Stage 4: Now one person plays one keyboard, in one piece with two halves.
- Stage 5: The meaning shifts again: one keyboard player (**keyboard sonata**). Scarlatti's K. 9 is one movement in two repeated halves (**binary form**). The first half moves away from the home key, the **tonic**, to a new key: in a major key usually the **dominant**; in a minor key like K. 9, often the relative major. The second half comes home. K. 9 is No. 9 of the 30 *Essercizi*, dedicated to João V of Portugal; the Garden dates the set 1738 to 39. Giustini's *Sonate da cimbalo di piano e forte* (Florence, 1732) came first: the Garden's card calls them the first sonatas published for the piano, which could play softer and louder by touch.
- Stage 6: The Garden's card says Scarlatti's *Essercizi per gravicembalo* were published in London in 1738 to 39, and were the only sonatas he published; Kirkpatrick's catalogue (1953) numbers 555. Their binary form is tonal: the first half modulates to the dominant or relative major, the second returns home, and the closing material of each half often matches, a rhyme that anticipates the recapitulation of sonata form. The card also says later composers joined several movements together; that lies beyond this journey.

**What changed since Chapter 2.**
- Stage 4 (Scarlatti only): A leap. No group of players now, just one keyboard player and one piece in two halves.
- Stage 5 (Giustini and Scarlatti): A leap: the sonata leaves the chamber group for a single keyboard player. Inside this chapter the plan differs: Giustini writes a sonata of several short movements; Scarlatti writes one movement in two repeated halves.
- Stage 6: A leap that changes instrument and number of players at once, with no continuo part to hold up the harmony: the keyboard player plays everything. Inside this chapter the number of movements changes: Giustini's sonata is five dance movements (Balletto, Corrente, Sarabanda, Giga, Minuet); Scarlatti's K. 9 is one movement in two halves, and its structure is a tonal one, a move away from the tonic and a return.

**1. 1732 · *Sonate da cimbalo di piano e forte*: Sonata No. 1, Lodovico Giustini.** *Stages 5 and 6.*
Recording: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DroR76BkVrk
Listen for:
- (structure) separate movements, each with its own speed and character.
- (dynamics) whether the player can make the sound softer and louder by touch.
Find out by listening:
- Stages 5 and 6: Is this a harpsichord or an early piano? How can you tell?
- Stage 5: How many separate movements can you hear? How does each one move?
- Stage 6: Mark where each movement begins. Compare the sound and shape of these movements with the single movement of Scarlatti's K. 9.
For teachers: The recording's performer and instrument are not identified. It is a personal upload titled "Sonata Nº 1 in G minor, for Cimbalo di Piano e Forte". The Garden's card describes it as played on fortepiano, but that is not confirmed, so do not tell students what instrument it uses. The recording has not been checked by ear, and it has not been confirmed that it plays all five movements. The Garden's linked score is the Op. 1 set on IMSLP.
*Notice (Stages 5 and 6):* in the next work, 6 years on, there is one movement, not several.

## What I learned

1. What stayed the same in every stop, whatever the word "sonata" meant?
2. What changed most between the first stop and the last: who plays it, how many movements, or how it is shaped?
3. Why do you think it changed: what did players, churches, courts or instruments need at each step?

**For teachers.** The honest answer to question 1 is "played by instruments, not sung". It is not "a continuo": Gabrieli's 1597 sonata has no written continuo part (an organist may have added chords) and the Scarlatti sonata has none.
